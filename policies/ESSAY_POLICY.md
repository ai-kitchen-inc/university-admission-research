ESSAY_POLICY.md (v2)
Rules for writing and drafting every essay in this folder (Common App, supplements, scholarship essays). These apply to anything Claude drafts and to Eidee's own revisions. If a draft breaks one of these, it gets fixed before it counts as done.

v2 note. Rules 1 to 6 are unchanged from v1 except for one scoping amendment inside rules 5 and 6, marked below. Rules 7 to 13 are new. They come from the Stanford v1 review board (18 Aug 2026), which scored that set 72.2 and traced almost all of the loss to five things this file did not previously say. Where v1 was silent, drafts filled the silence with a house style; the new rules name what the house style was getting wrong.


1. Voice: a real human, native English, high-school level
Write as Eidee would: a sharp, reflective 17-year-old native English speaker, not an academic or a marketing department.
Favor plain, concrete words over fancy ones. If a simpler word works, use it.
Vary sentence length. Short sentences are allowed and good. Some fragments are fine when they land.
Contractions are fine (it's, I'd, don't). They sound human.
The final essay must be Eidee's own words. Drafts are scaffolds to react to and rewrite, never to submit as-is. Read every line aloud; if it doesn't sound like you, change it.
2. No double hyphens, and watch the AI tells
Never use "--" (double hyphen). It reads as machine-generated and signals "AI wrote this" to admissions readers.
Go easy on the em dash too. Prefer a period, a comma, a colon, or parentheses. If a sentence leans on dashes to hold itself together, rewrite it.
Avoid the common AI tell-words and crutches: delve, tapestry, testament, multifaceted, navigate (figuratively), moreover, furthermore, in conclusion, "it's not just X, it's Y" as a reflex, and the endless rule-of-three list ("curiosity, creativity, and courage"). Use sparingly and only when true.
Avoid robotic symmetry (every paragraph the same length, every idea in threes). Real writing is a little uneven.
No emoji. No headers inside an essay. No bullet points inside an essay.
3. No empty superlatives. Ground every claim in specifics.
Ban phrases like "world-class university," "prestigious institution," "amazing opportunities," "top-tier education." They're tiring and say nothing; readers skim past them.
Replace the superlative with the specific reason. Name the actual program, professor, course, tradition, value, or feature, and connect it to something true about you.
Each school has its own character. Anchor "why us" essays in that character, for example:
Yale: residential-college community, "light and truth," dialogue across difference, the BA/MM with the School of Music, specific faculty clusters (perception/attention/memory, music cognition).
MIT: "mind and hand," maker culture, doing things for the pleasure of it, collaboration.
Stanford: intellectual vitality, "the wind of freedom blows," self-directed exploration.
Princeton: service and civic purpose, undergraduate focus, the senior thesis.
Harvard: breadth, citizen-leaders, the Harvard/NEC path for a musician.
Brown: the Open Curriculum and intellectual self-direction.
Test for every sentence in a "why school" essay: could this sentence be copied into an essay for a different school? If yes, it's too generic. Make it impossible to transplant.
v2 addition. The specifics come from the university dossier at https://this-goober.github.io/ (local copy: this-goober.github.io/brochures/), where every decision-driving fact is already verified against an official source and cited. Use it rather than memory. Its "honest risks" section says what each school does not offer; never write an aspiration it contradicts.
4. Tell it like a film (the Spielberg rule)
A great essay is a story, not a summary. Borrow structure from film and from how the best investors read pitches: they want a compelling arc, and the arc is rarely a straight line of wins.
Build the shape: context (a real moment, a setup), then tension or a problem, then the struggle and the attempts, then real difficulty including despair or failure, then the turn, then what it meant. The failures and the pivots are not weaknesses to hide; they are what make the story land.
Show decisions and turning points: why you pivoted, why you chose one path over another, what you learned when something broke. A learning or a failure, told honestly, often beats a clean success.
Use scenes, sensory detail, and specific moments instead of abstractions. One vivid, true scene is worth a paragraph of summary.
Earn the emotion. A triumphant ending only moves a reader if they felt the risk and the doubt first.
5. Start from the big dream, then work backward (the pitch-deck rule)
Open with the audacious vision, the "impossible" version of what should exist, not a cautious account of what you have today. Don't shrink the dream to fit current resources or what seems realistic.
Then do the honest backward work: the experiments, the attempts, the trials, the people you consulted, the things you tried and dropped, the faith you had to find. The vision earns credibility only when it is connected, step by step, to real effort and real evidence.
This pairs with rule 4: the vision is the destination; the attempts, struggles, and pivots are the story that makes the destination believable. Big dream up front, grounded proof underneath.
Avoid the opposite failure mode: a vision with no backward path reads as fantasy; a backward path with no vision reads as a chore list. You need both.
v2 amendment (scope). This rule applies to essays whose prompt can carry altitude. It is suspended in intimate prompts (a note to a roommate, a letter to a person) and in inventory prompts (five things, how you spent your summers). See rule 7.
6. Raise the Lid: write from vision, never from credentials
Frame from the future, not the resume. Open from the problem worth a life, or the world that should exist, then ground backward to small, real fixes already made. The frame is the ceiling; choose the largest true one.
The visionary register. Write like someone whose attention is on what is right and what must eventually exist, not on proving what they are today. A high-schooler is allowed to think at that altitude.
No credential-drops. Never name a self-made title or program to validate yourself ("I founded," "as president of"). It is already on the resume; inside an essay it reads as insecurity and lowers the Lid. If an activity belongs, show the work as a scene, do not announce the badge.
No self-downgrade, no hedges. Cut "the most useful thing I own isn't...," "lucky enough to," "in my small way," "I had the opportunity to." Earned confidence assumes your ideas matter.
Vulnerability is not insecurity. Keep honest struggles, failures, and growth edges at full weight. What you cut is the insecurity register, not the honesty.
v2 amendment (scope, and one exception). The Lid applies where a frame is legible. It is suspended in intimate and inventory prompts (rule 7). And where a prompt literally asks about a program, a place, or a summer, naming it is answering the question, not credential-dropping. Legitimate names stay; the compliance note must list them rather than claim there are none.
v2 addition (the flinch). Stopping one sentence short of the frame you earned is worse than framing modestly from the start. If the body of an essay does the work to reach a larger idea, the last sentence has to land there. Read the final sentence cold and ask what the essay was about; if the answer is smaller than what the body proved, the last sentence is wrong.


7. Register: match the prompt's genre, not one house style (new)
Rules 5 and 6 are right for essays that can carry altitude and wrong for the ones that cannot. Applied to a note to a future roommate, they produce a memo: numbered points, a thesis, an academic aside, a condition at the end. That is not a bold choice, it is the single most expensive error available, because several schools cap the authenticity score outright for a guarded, formal register in an informal prompt.

Assign a register before writing:

Intimate (roommate note, a letter to a person): talking, not writing. Lid off. Address the reader as a person and be curious about them. One concrete accident a camera could photograph. One concession that actually costs you. No thesis, no numbered structure, no mention of your intended major, no abstract noun in the last sentence. End on an offer, a question, or an image, never on a condition.
Intellectual ("an idea that excites you"): thinking out loud. Lid on. Show the thinking, do not report it.
Position ("the challenge society faces", what you believe): a conviction with a route. Lid on but routed through something you personally built or saw.
Inventory (five things, two summers): flat, deliberately unedited. Lid off. Things and habits, not virtues. At least one item nobody would put there to impress.
Contribution ("what would you contribute"): an operating habit shown at work, not claimed. Lid on, but the last line names a person or a room, not a principle.
Fit (why us, why this major): a continuation of work already underway. Named specifics from the dossier, each attached to something you have already done.

A set written entirely in one register reads as performed, even when every piece is true.
8. Cold drafting (new — formalizes what was already practice)
Claude's drafts supply facts, structure, transitions, and shape. Descriptive, sensory and emotional language is Eidee's. Where a scene is needed and the files do not contain it, the draft does not invent it and does not leave a bare blank. It states a requisition: the shape of the scene, its length, what it must contain, what it must not do, and the specific way it could go wrong.

And the hard rule: a placeholder may not carry an essay. If deleting every placeholder leaves the piece hollow, the piece is not a draft yet. The v1 roommate note outsourced its entire warmth budget to three empty parentheses, and with them removed there was nothing warm left. That is what capped it.
9. No maxim without a route (new)
An insight that arrives finished reads as a conclusion inherited from somewhere. An insight the reader watches being formed reads as a mind.

Put the thing that changed your mind before the conclusion, always. A conclusion followed by its justification is a position being defended; a falsifier followed by a conclusion is a person thinking.
The strongest available move is the sentence where your own hypothesis loses. Use it where you have one.
If a piece contains no turn, it does not get a maxim. It ends on the concrete thing.
Applies at every length. A fifty-word answer cannot carry a full arc, but it can carry one object that is yours instead of one opinion that is everyone's.

The keynote test. Read any sentence of conviction in the voice of a well-known speaker. If it sounds at home there, it is not yours yet, however true it is. Route it through something you have touched.
10. Don't repeat a rhetorical move (new)
One construction may appear at most twice across a whole school's set, and at most once in any single piece. The repeat offender is the antithetical couplet: "the danger isn't X, it's Y," "not a cause, a habit," "I thought I was building X, I was building Y," "that looks like quitting, it is not." Each one is good. Six in seven hundred words is a template, and readers now associate the pattern with generated text.

Also budgeted: three-item lists, one-word fragments for emphasis, repeated sentence openings, and the staccato inventory. Keep the best instance, rewrite the rest as ordinary sentences.
11. Let the reader see something (new)
Across a school's whole set: at least one line of actual quoted speech, at least three things a camera could photograph, and at least one sound. At least one physical anchor in each long essay.

This is easy to fail without noticing. A set can be full of precise numbers, dates and mechanisms and still contain no room, no voice and no sound, so a reader finishes it without having seen anything. Numbers prove the work happened; anchors make it present. Both are scored, and for a musician submitting a performance portfolio, a set where nobody can hear anything is a strange silence.

Proportionality: spend this in the long pieces. A fifty-word answer should stay dense.
12. Never brag through a flaw (new)
Any time a flaw sits next to an achievement, the pairing is structurally a humblebrag, and research finds humblebrags are liked less than straight statements and less than straight complaints. It survives only under four conditions:

The flaw is the subject; the achievement is scenery. If the paragraph builds toward the achievement, it is a brag in a costume.
Never characterize the quality of your own work. Cut "flawlessly," "perfectly," and every synonym. The instrument enters as an object you left somewhere, not as evidence.
Never write the "and yet" sentence. Naming the contrast is what converts it into self-promotion. Let the reader do the arithmetic; they will, and they will enjoy having done it.
It has to cost you something. If the flaw is one you are secretly proud of, a reader on essay number forty will smell it.
13. Verify before you build, not after (new)
Two tiers.

Blocking. Any number or fact that a piece leans on, any cross-document dependency (which personal statement a school receives; what another school's set already claims), any institutional claim. Resolve these before drafting. Do not build three essays on an assumption and flag the assumption at the end.
Trailing. Cosmetic detail that changes a word rather than a structure. May ship with a draft.

No unverified number may be the spine of an essay. If a figure carries a piece, either confirm it first or write the sentence that works without it and keep both.

No blanket negatives in a compliance note. Every claim is either a quoted instance or a search that actually returned zero. Claiming "no program names anywhere" while two answers name institutions is the kind of error that makes a reader distrust the rest of the note.


Quick pre-submit checklist
Sounds like Eidee read aloud; no sentence sounds like a brochure or a bot.
Zero "--". Em dashes rare. No AI tell-words.
No empty superlatives; every "why school" line names something specific from the dossier and non-transplantable.
Register matches each prompt's genre. The roommate note is a person talking, not a memo.
There's a real arc: setup, tension, struggle, a failure or pivot, a turn, meaning.
Every conviction has a route. Nothing in it could be a keynote line.
No rhetorical move used more than twice across the set.
At least one line of speech, three photographable things, and one sound across the set.
Any flaw-next-to-achievement passes all four humblebrag conditions.
The frame is the largest true one, and the last sentence lands on it.
No title-drops, no hedges, no self-downgrade. Vulnerability stays, insecurity doesn't.
Every blocking fact verified before drafting; no unverified number is holding up an essay.
The compliance note quotes evidence instead of asserting absence.
